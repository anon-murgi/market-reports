What is a Williams fractal?

Bill Williams, a trader, named a simple five-candle shape. It marks a small local peak or dip on a price chart.

  1. Fractal high: the middle candle's high sits above the highs of the two candles before it and the two after it.
  2. Fractal low: the same idea flipped. The middle candle's low sits below its four neighbours.
  3. You only know it two days late. The shape needs two more candles to the right, so it gets confirmed at the close of the fifth candle.
  4. The trading idea: when price later closes above a confirmed fractal high, buy, and put the stop-loss under the latest fractal low.

The fractal rule, in plain words

Everything below was fixed in writing before the test ran. Both rules on this page share every line except the first one.

When to buy
A day closes above the fractal high (plus 0.1%)
The fractal high stays usable for 20 trading days. Only the first close above it counts. The plain rule uses "close above the 20-day high" here instead.
Only in uptrends
Stock and market must both trend up
The stock's price sits above its 50-day average, which sits above its 200-day average and is rising. The Nifty 50 must sit above its own 200-day average.
How to buy
At the next morning's opening price
If the stock opens too far above the signal (more than half a day's typical range), the order is cancelled.
Safety net
Stop-loss just under the latest fractal low
The stop must sit 2% to 8% below the buy price. Closer or wider, and the trade gets skipped.
How much to buy
Risk 0.5% of the account per trade
If the stop gets hit, the account loses about 0.5%. No more than 10% of the money in one stock, 10 stocks at once, and 3% total risk.
When to sell
A trailing stop that only moves up
The stop follows the highest close since buying, minus 3 times the stock's average daily range (ATR). Any trade still open after 60 trading days gets sold.
Only busy stocks
At least ₹5 crore traded per day
Measured as the median over the last 60 days. Each trade uses at most 0.1% of that.
Costs
0.3% on every buy and every sell
Brokerage, taxes and slippage together, so 0.6% for a round trip. Stress tests double it.

    The pass test: 11 hurdles, one missed

    Before the test, the study wrote down what "good enough to paper trade" means. The fractal rule cleared 10 of the 11 money and code hurdles on the 2021 to 2025 final exam. It missed the one that asks whether the profit could be luck. Separately, the data itself does not meet the audit standard, so the study could never have promoted the rule anyway.

    How sure can we be?

    The study reshuffled the trade history 5,000 times (a method called bootstrapping) to see how much the average result moves around by chance. The bars show the range that covers 95% of those reshuffles. A bar that crosses the zero line means the result could plausibly be zero or negative.

    What the buy and sell codes mean
    Ways to buy
    E0: buy the moment price touches the fractal high, no filter.
    E1: same, but only above the Alligator "teeth" line (a slow moving average Williams used).
    E2: same, but only in uptrends.
    E3: wait for a daily close above the fractal high, uptrends only (the fractal rule).
    C20 / C55: close above the 20-day / 55-day high, uptrends only.
    Ways to sell
    X0: stop climbs under each new fractal low.
    X1: stop trails 3 ATR under the highest close (used by both main rules).
    X2: fixed target at 2R profit, stop stays put.
    All exits also sell after 60 trading days.

    Words used on this page

    Candle
    One day of trading for one stock: its open, high, low and close price.
    Breakout
    Price moving above a level it had not crossed recently. Here, the level is a fractal high or a 20-day high.
    Stop-loss
    A price where you sell to cap your loss if the trade goes wrong.
    Trailing stop
    A stop-loss that moves up as the price rises, and never moves down. It locks in some profit.
    ATR (average true range)
    How much a stock typically moves in a day, averaged over 14 days. A wide-swinging stock has a big ATR.
    R (risk unit)
    What a trade stood to lose if the stop-loss got hit. A +2R trade made twice its risk; a -1R trade lost exactly its risk.
    Yearly growth (CAGR)
    The steady yearly return that would turn the start value into the end value. 4.7% a year turns ₹10 lakh into about ₹12.6 lakh over five years.
    Win rate
    Share of trades that made money after costs.
    Profit factor
    Money made on winners divided by money lost on losers. Above 1 means overall profit; 1.31 means ₹1.31 earned per ₹1 lost.
    Worst fall (max drawdown)
    The biggest drop in account value from a previous peak, before it recovered.
    Invested share (exposure)
    The average fraction of the account held in stocks rather than cash.
    Basis point (bp)
    One hundredth of a percent. 30 bps = 0.3%.
    Bootstrap range
    A range built by reshuffling past results thousands of times. It shows how much a number could move by luck alone.
    Final exam (reserved test)
    Years kept aside until the rules were frozen, so nobody can tune the rules to fit them.
    Survivor bias
    Testing only on companies alive today. Failed companies drop out, so past results look better than they were.
    Adjusted price
    Past prices rescaled for splits, bonuses and dividends. Good for charts, but nobody traded at those exact rupee levels.
    Nifty 50 / Nifty 500
    Indexes of India's 50 and 500 largest listed companies. An index fund that tracks them is the simplest alternative to any strategy.
    Slots
    The 10 places in the account for open trades. A new trade needs a free slot.
    Placebo test
    Running the same rules on scrambled prices. Profit that survives the scramble did not need the real price history.
    Exposure-matched index
    A Nifty 50 fund held at the same daily invested share as the strategy, with the rest in cash. It carries the same market risk, so it makes a fair comparison.
    Split break
    A jump in an adjusted price series where a share split was not rescaled. The stock appears to multiply in value overnight when nothing happened.

    What would make this study stronger

    None of these can rescue the verdict. They would make the "no" firmer, or turn it into a clean "yes" on better data.
